Optimising for: the act of sending one. The box is the screen, and everything under it is the receipt.

Read from the built backend (f13dd81..f845c4e), not assumed GET /api/v1/suggestions returns {id, body, status, decision_note, decided_at, created_at} and nothing else, newest first by created_at — never by status. A decided one sits above one still in review whenever it was sent later, which is exactly what the sample below shows. Any grouping by state is the client doing it, not the endpoint. There is no reviewer name to draw. decided_by is stored and is not in the author's payload, so the answer is EzBizi's word rather than a named person's. Drawing "Rob at EzBizi" would be drawing a field that never arrives. 2000 characters, and an empty body is refused with "Write the suggestion first." Nobody at your own organization can read this — not an administrator, not the owner. SuggestionsController filters user_id on every path in addition to the tenant scope, and the module deliberately writes no audit row, because an audit line saying "Marisol sent a suggestion" would publish the existence, timing and frequency of every private one to exactly the people it is kept from. That fact belongs on the screen: a channel nobody believes is private is a channel nobody uses.
The one rule that has to be visible rather than described Edit and Withdraw exist on a new one and on nothing else. The server refuses both the moment somebody starts reading — an author may correct or take back a suggestion only while nobody has read it. So the three later states must not merely lack the buttons; they have to say why the buttons are gone, or the screen looks broken to somebody who used them yesterday. In all three layouts the footer row survives and the buttons are replaced by the sentence:
  • In review — "Somebody at EzBizi is reading this one, so it is fixed while they do."
  • Approved / Denied — "This one has been answered, so it stays as it was written."
Both are the server's own words from SuggestionsController::frozen(), which returns them as a 422 to anybody who reaches the endpoint another way.

1 · Compose first The box is at the top, always, at full width, with the counter and Send under it. Below it one heading and a plain stack of what you have sent, newest first, each carrying its state and — where there is one — the answer. It reads as a suggestion box, which is what it is, and the first visit needs no explaining because the thing you came to do is the first thing on the screen and is already open. What it trades away: once you have twenty of them, the box you use twice a year owns the best space on the screen forever, and there is no way to see only the ones still waiting — you scroll. It also pushes the newest answer below the fold on a short window, and that answer is the one thing a returning visitor came for.

Desktop — your suggestions, all four states

Look at the footer row of each card. Edit and Withdraw are on the New one. On the other three the same row is still there and carries a sentence instead — the absence is stated, not silent. And look at the order: New, Approved, In review, Denied. That is created_at descending and nothing else, which is what the endpoint returns.

Desktop — the first visit, which is what most people will see

No list, so the heading and the explanation stand where the list would be and the box is open under them. Send is outlined rather than filled while the box is empty — not faded: the label stays at full contrast, because the rule against fading an element that carries text applies hardest to the state that has something to say about itself.

Desktop — correcting one, in place

Edit turns the card's body into the same box, with the same counter and the same limit, and a Cancel beside Save. No dialog — constraint 1, and there is nothing a panel would do here that the row the text already occupies does not.

375px — designed for the phone, not narrowed

Composing is its own screen, and Send lives in the bar. On a phone the keyboard takes roughly half the screen, so a Send under a text box sits behind the keyboard at the exact moment you are typing into it. The list screen therefore leads with one wide button rather than an open box; the box is one tap away and, once open, has nothing else on it.
